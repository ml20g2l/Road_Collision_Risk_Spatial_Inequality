# Analytical Findings and Decision Implications

## Executive conclusion

The project finds a persistent spatial inequality in collision occurrence, a separate rural-severity problem, and a meaningful—but ranking-stable—effect from injury-based severity adjustment. These patterns support a two-track intervention strategy: reduce collision occurrence in deprived urban communities while investigating high-severity rural road contexts separately.

The findings are descriptive. They identify where deeper investigation and intervention design should be prioritised; they do not establish causal effects.

## 1. Collision burden is persistently higher in deprived communities

Across 2021–2025, the most deprived English LSOAs recorded 187.7 collisions per 100,000 residents per year, compared with 92.5 in the least deprived decile. The resulting 2.03x ratio was not driven by one abnormal year: the annual most-to-least-deprived ratio remained between 1.98x and 2.09x.

This is a strong and persistent concentration signal. It supports prioritising deprived areas for place-based road-safety diagnosis, but the resident denominator must not be interpreted as traffic exposure. The pattern may reflect differences in road design, traffic mix, walking exposure, land use, reporting, or other unmeasured factors.

## 2. The inequality is stronger in occurrence than in conditional severity

Adjusted expected KSI was 23.9 per 100 collisions in the most deprived decile and 25.3 in the least deprived decile. The deprivation gradient in total burden is therefore not explained by collisions becoming more severe after they occur.

The operational implication is important: an intervention focused only on trauma response or post-collision severity would miss the principal inequality signal. The first diagnostic priority should be the conditions associated with collision occurrence.

## 3. Rural roads present a distinct severity problem

In 2025, rural areas recorded 31.7 adjusted expected KSI per 100 collisions, compared with 24.3 in urban areas. The rural value was consistently around 30–32 across the study period, while the urban value was around 21–24.

This result argues against a single national intervention ranking. Urban locations require attention to collision frequency and social concentration; rural locations require a stronger focus on the consequences of each collision, emergency response, road geometry, and speed environment.

## 4. Speed limit is informative but not sufficient

Across 2021–2025, adjusted expected KSI per 100 collisions was 20.4 on 20 mph roads, 23.6 on 30 mph roads, 26.8 on 40 mph roads, 29.7 on 50 mph roads, and 36.5 on 60 mph roads. The value then fell to 25.4 on 70 mph roads.

The non-monotonic result shows why speed limit should not be treated as a causal explanation on its own. Motorway design, traffic separation, junction density, vulnerable-road-user exposure, and rural single-carriageway geometry are likely confounders. The next analytical extension should model road class, junction context, road-user type, and urban/rural setting jointly.

## 5. Severity adjustment changes levels more than priorities

The injury-based adjustment raised the national KSI rate by 6.0% in 2021 and 2.1% in 2025. Despite this change in level, the 2025 local-authority raw and adjusted risk rankings had a Spearman correlation of 0.991.

The adjusted measure is the better default for comparison, while the raw value should remain visible for transparency. The near-identical ranking means broad geographic priorities are robust, but performance targets and year-on-year statements can still be sensitive to the chosen severity definition.

## Recommended decision framework

1. Use adjusted expected KSI per billion vehicle miles for local-authority risk comparison.
2. Use resident-normalised collision rates only to describe community burden, with a visible exposure caveat.
3. Separate urban collision-frequency interventions from rural severity interventions.
4. Show raw and adjusted KSI together so measurement effects remain auditable.
5. Keep AADF point matching as a sensitivity diagnostic until road-segment geometry supports defensible link-level exposure.
